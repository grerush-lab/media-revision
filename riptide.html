Music Videos — Riptide (Vance Joy, 2013)

Debut single from Vance Joy's EP God Loves You When You're Dancing, later featured on Dream Your Life Away (2014). A low-budget, surrealist/avant-garde influenced video — studied for Media Language and Representation. Remember: the exam's Media Language question always uses an unseen music video, so Representation is what's actually examinable on Riptide itself.

Exam focus: Media Language below covers Goodwin, intertextuality, genre theory and avant-garde/surrealist influence — essential revision vocabulary, but the exam question on an unseen text. Representation — especially gender — is where Riptide itself will actually come up.

Context in brief

The product

  • Written and produced by Australian singer-songwriter Vance Joy (James Keogh) with drummer Edwin White; released 2013, alternative/indie folk genre.
  • Commercially huge: number 1 in Triple J's Hottest 100 (2013), UK top 10, number 1 on Billboard's Alternative Songs chart, and multiple APRA/ASCAP songwriting awards.
  • Low-budget, artistic production rather than the typical big-budget, effects-driven video — a deliberate choice that may have helped the song stand out, or simply reflected a new artist's limited resources at the time.
  • Mushroom Music/independent label roots meant a smaller budget than a major-label pop act — Vance Joy's later video for "Georgia" shows the shift to bigger production once he had commercial success behind him.

Industry point worth remembering: a music video's budget can itself be read as a representation of how much a label values an artist — a label spending heavily signals confidence in a act's commercial pull, while a stripped-back video (like Riptide) can either reflect low investment, or be a deliberate strategy to court audiences who value authenticity over polish.

Media Language

Codes, editing & Goodwin's theory

  • Andrew Goodwin: many of Riptide's images relate directly to the lyrics (a woman running is shown for "lady running down to the riptide"), but Goodwin also notes videos are often polysemic — open to more than one reading.
  • Overt montage editing: contrasting, juxtaposing shots are hard-cut together, often with no logical link to each other — only a link to the lyrics or a mood, not a continuous narrative.
  • Eisenstein — intellectual montage: colliding unrelated images (a couple looking off-screen, then a cut to a pile of dollar bills) generates new meaning through juxtaposition rather than through a linear story.
  • Roland Barthes — semiotics: the dollar bills are a simple sign to decode, and connect to the lyric "all my friends are turning green" — green connoting both money and envy, implying friends who envy Joy's success or who have themselves "sold out".

Avant-garde & Surrealist intertextuality

  • Avant-garde: an art movement pushing against accepted norms. Riptide challenges typical music-video expectations by refusing a clear narrative or performance structure.
  • Surrealist cinema (Paris, 1920s) used shocking, irrational imagery to challenge art's usual job of representing reality — visible in Riptide's disconnected, dreamlike sequence of images.
  • The video's shot of a woman's hand being "sliced" directly references the notorious opening of Un Chien Andalou (Buñuel & Dalí, 1929), where an eye is sliced with a razor — both reject chronological, traditional narrative.
  • Intertextual lyric references: "there's a movie that I think you'll like, this cowboy decides to quit his job and head to New York City" nods to Midnight Cowboy (1969); "closest thing to Michelle Pfeiffer" alludes to her glamorous 1980s star image (e.g. Scarface) to imply the girl's beauty.
  • Per Goodwin, intertextual reference is used to engage audiences and reward recognition — viewers who catch the film references get a small hit of gratification.

Genre — does Riptide adhere or subvert?

Some argue Riptide adheres to convention: it has a clear lyric-visual relationship, intertextual referencing, and mixes narrative/conceptual modes as Goodwin describes. Others argue it subverts convention: its indie/alternative genre isn't reflected in any consistent visual style, it is not performance-based, and there are almost no close-ups of Joy himself — so star persona is barely used, making it hard to "put a face to the song". The opening shot even includes a billing block normally seen in film trailers, borrowing a cinematic register from the start.

Multiple genres are blended for effect: comedy (a humorous, self-deprecating lyric — "I was scared of pretty girls and starting conversations" — over a beach scene), horror/thriller (a woman with a torch in a graveyard at night), and western (a cowboy figure and desert iconography, echoing the lyric "cowboy running from south"). Steve Neale's genre theory helps explain this: genres rely on repetition and difference — enough familiar signifiers to be recognisable, combined with novelty, to keep an audience engaged and unsure what to expect next.

Riptide can be read as both adhering to and subverting music video convention, and a strong answer would hold both readings rather than picking one. It adheres to Goodwin's theory through its clear (if often surreal) links between lyrics and visuals and its heavy use of intertextual reference. But it subverts genre expectation by refusing a clear performance mode or star persona — we rarely see Joy's face — and by blending genre signifiers (horror, western, comedy) that don't map onto a single, coherent visual identity, which arguably reflects the avant-garde and Surrealist influences on its editing style.

Representation

Gender — powerlessness & the female body

  • The woman in the video is repeatedly shown running, barefoot, at night, and being dragged away into darkness (matching the lyric "taken away to the darkside") — visually reinforcing a lack of power and agency.
  • Scenes of a woman's hand being "sliced" and a bleeding neck she doesn't react to work to trivialise violence against women — the injuries are shown almost decoratively, without the narrative weight real violence would carry.
  • bell hooks — feminist theory: women are consistently framed as objects in danger — tortured, watched, "amputated" — which some readings see as reinforcing patriarchal ideas of female vulnerability, while others see it as a knowing mockery of exactly those Hammer-horror-style conventions (compare Kiss of the Vampire's "damsel" trope).
  • David Gauntlett — pick 'n' mix identity: the video's fragmented structure invites an active audience to construct their own reading rather than handing over one fixed message — for example, deciding for themselves whether the woman is a captive fleeing danger, or something more metaphorical.

The male gaze — and where it's complicated

  • Laura Mulvey — the male gaze: a woman is shown seductively slipping the strap from her shoulder as the camera zooms in — positioning her as an object to be watched, aligning the camera (and audience) with a desiring male viewpoint.
  • The video literalises this theory with a prop — a book titled "Techniques of Photographing Girls" — held in frame, an almost knowing wink at how women are packaged for the camera in media generally.
  • But a woman is also shown holding binoculars, actively looking rather than only being looked at — complicating a straightforward male-gaze reading and raising the question of whether the video critiques voyeurism or simply reproduces it.
  • Elsewhere, a man raises his arms triumphantly to the sky — a more traditionally masculine, unselfconscious pose that contrasts with how the women in the video are framed.
Applying Mulvey, several shots position women as objects to be watched — most explicitly the strap-slipping shot alongside the "Techniques of Photographing Girls" book, which almost parodies the male gaze by naming it outright. However, the video complicates a purely Mulvey-driven reading by also showing a woman actively looking through binoculars, which momentarily reverses who holds the gaze. A strong exam-style answer would hold both readings in tension rather than settling on one, using bell hooks and Gauntlett to argue the video's fragmented, polysemic structure leaves it open to being read as either reinforcing or critiquing how women are represented.

A public/private sphere reading

The video can be read as reinforcing a patriarchal world view: women are shown sleeping, running, or being dragged into darkness, which can symbolically suggest they have no real voice in the public sphere and are repeatedly pulled back into a private, domestic, powerless "place". Whether this is a critique of that world view or simply a reproduction of it is a genuinely open debate — exactly the kind of evaluative tension examiners want to see explored rather than resolved into a single tidy answer.

Epic Games, Technology & Convergence

Epic Games — the fact drill

  • First commercial release: ZZT (1991) — Fortnite was NOT their first game; they also made the Gears of War series.
  • Unreal Engine (1998; Unreal Engine 4 in 2014) — one of the most successful game engines ever, used for Batman: Arkham Asylum, Mortal Kombat, Infinity Blade… and Fortnite. Epic makes it available on its website so audiences can develop their own games.
  • The Epic Games Store launched December 2018 — selling other games (Cyberpunk, Borderlands) that all profit the same company.

Technological convergence

Fortnite can be played on PS4, Xbox One, Nintendo Switch, iOS, Android, Windows and Mac — and players can move mid-game between devices without interrupting gameplay: at home, on the move, tiny screen or projector.

That's technological convergence — technology coming together in one place. Video games + smartphones brought gaming to the most-used media tool in the world.

Cross-media convergence & synergy — Fortnite's superpower

Cross-media convergence: different media brands or products forming links to promote each other. Synergy: brands joining to mutually benefit. Fortnite's format means any costume, weapon or game can be dropped in — keeping the game fresh AND in the news.

  • Stranger Things (July 2019): Chief Hopper and Demogorgon skin wraps, themed items, and in-game portals identical to the show's — TV and gaming promoting each other.
  • Celebrities: Epic's relationships with Drake and NBA star Ben Simmons promote the game beyond the traditional gaming market.
  • Streaming: Fortnite is the most-viewed game on YouTube, and uses Twitch (owned by Amazon) to broadcast live competitions.

The Travis Scott concert & the metaverse

During the in-game "Astronomical" event, a giant Travis Scott performed around the island while players raced across the map to watch — music + gaming + live event in one product.

Fortnite is often described as the start of the metaverse: a shared virtual space where audiences don't just play, but socialise, watch and attend events.

03 · Industry

Funding & Profit — How Fortnite Makes Money

Fortnite follows a GaaS model — Games as a Service. Battle Royale is free at the point of sale: the revenue comes almost entirely from what happens after you start playing.

$1.2bn
Made within 10 months of release
200m
Registered players worldwide by April 2019 (125m in the first year)
~70%
Of players make in-game purchases (LendEDU survey)
$3m
Generated in the first 24 hours of Season 5's launch

The revenue streams

  • Microtransactions — small in-game purchases (weapons, skins, emotes) bought with V-Bucks, the in-game currency exchanged for real money. By July 2018 these had generated ~$1 billion; nearly all of Fortnite's ~$1.8bn 2019 revenue came from them.
  • A daily storefront — a new "wardrobe" of skins for sale every day; through the work of costume designers, fashion is where the game really makes its money.
  • Battle Pass — costs the equivalent of ~$9.50 in V-Bucks; popular with players who don't have the time or patience to earn everything through gameplay. Bonus V-Buck deals encourage buying more than you need.
  • Save the World — a paid mode: £34.99 standard / £49.99 deluxe, with occasional 50%-off discounts to widen the audience.
  • Live events — Fortnite Fan Festival, World Cup Final: tickets from ~$50.
  • Merchandise — hoodies, T-shirts, caps, skateboards, pop vinyl, onesies… plus even paid one-on-one coaching sessions.
  • The Epic Games Store — the website funnels players to other games (Cyberpunk, Borderlands) that profit the same company.
Skins: cosmetic, "insignificant" purchases — vast sums for Epic

Why it works — the success formula

  • Free to play = no £50 barrier · constant events and near-daily updates keep it fresh · cartoon, non-threatening graphics widen the age range.
  • Cross-platform availability maximises accessibility and revenue potential.
  • E-sports and tournaments raise visibility; celebrity endorsements (Drake + Ninja pulled 500K+ viewers on the spur of a moment) attract new users.
  • You can unlock rewards by playing — but the Battle Pass makes daily play (and spending) feel necessary.
  • Its success pushed the whole industry toward the Battle Royale genre — and remember the market is competitive: Fortnite's earnings dropped 38% between May 2018 and May 2019.

04 · Industry

Regulation — PEGI

PEGI = Pan European Game Information ("pan-European" = Europe-wide). Its aim: the protection of minors from harmful video game content. You will only be asked about PEGI in the exam.

Who regulates what

  • PEGI creates the age ratings used in over 30 countries.
  • In 2012 the PEGI system became UK law, and the Video Standards Council (VSC) was appointed the statutory body responsible for age-rating games in the UK using PEGI.
  • The Games Rating Authority (GRA) — a sub-arm of the VSC — specifically licenses the release of 12-, 16- and 18-rated games in the UK.
  • Other territories have their own regulators (Germany's USK; North America's ESRB) — but the exam asks about PEGI.

The classifications

  • Five age ratings: 3 · 7 · 12 · 16 · 18.
  • Eight content descriptors: violence · bad language · fear · gambling · sex · drugs · discrimination · in-game purchases.

Fortnite's rating — and the real worry

  • Fortnite is rated PEGI 12 on all consoles — for mild violence and in-game purchases.
  • Its violence is mild compared to Call of Duty or God of War — parents' bigger concerns are players' ability to spend real money and the game's potential for addiction, especially in younger players.
  • Lesson: different audiences interpret media products — and their appropriateness — in different ways.
  • Regulation flashpoints: Apple and Google removed Fortnite from their app stores (the Epic v Apple legal battle over payment systems); China restricted the game; and in June 2018 the WHO classified gaming addiction as a medical disorder, with the first UK patients treated on the NHS.
The in-game purchases descriptor — one of PEGI's eight content classifications, and the one that defines Fortnite

Uses & Gratifications (Blumler & Katz — PIES)

  • Personal Identity — the competitive "Victory Royale" affirms your status as a skilled gamer; role models from Ninja to Drake.
  • Information — learning strategies, watching streamers, mastering the meta; the website's updates and guides.
  • Entertainment — escapism: immersion in a world away from daily stresses; catharsis: releasing built-up emotions in a healthy way; the addictive "one more game" structure.
  • Social Relationships — voice-chat with squads, playing with friends, communities, live events — gaming as a social activity.

Reception theory (Stuart Hall) applied

  • Preferred: a player familiar with the Battle Royale format enjoys the fun, free, social game exactly as Epic intends.
  • Negotiated: a parent accepts it's creative and social, but worries about spending and screen time.
  • Oppositional: a critic sees a manipulative machine designed to extract money from children and keep them addicted.

Different audiences interpret the same product — and its appropriateness — in different ways.

Active vs Passive — and the Effects Debate

Active audiences select, engage, interpret and create their own meanings. Passive audiences accept messages without challenge. Fortnite is the perfect case study for both — a classic 8–12 mark question.

Sort it: evidence of an ACTIVE or PASSIVE audience?

Fortnite: Creative lets players design their own skins, emotes, landscapes and games
Epic releases Unreal Engine so audiences can develop their own games and shape the industry
Battle Royale requires strategy, problem-solving, communication and collaboration with other players
Battle Royale's repetitive structure stops challenging the player after a certain point
The addictive structure encourages playing the same levels again and again
Buying V-Bucks lets players skip challenges instead of earning rewards through gameplay
Players enter tournaments and engage with the community on the official website
The simple narrative (especially Battle Royale) leaves little room for interpreting themes and ideas

The Hypodermic Needle Model (1920s)

  • One of the first attempts to explain how audiences react to mass media: audiences passively receive "injected" messages without processing or challenging them.
  • Considered mostly obsolete — it doesn't allow for resistance or rejection, and is criticised as elitist and simplistic.
  • But it's still quoted during moral panics about violent games and films (the Bulger case, the Bobo doll experiment, Call of Duty's "No Russian" mission).

The concerns — and the defence

  • Concerns: online age ratings are hard to police · "no-skin" pressure pushes children into purchases · in-game chat has exposed children to predatory strangers · fantasy violence and competitiveness may encourage aggression (a "gateway game") · addictive design can crowd out sleep, study and socialising — Prince Harry publicly said it was designed to addict children.
  • Defence: the violence is mild and cartoon-like compared to GTA or Call of Duty · random free skins mean less racism/misogyny than many games · gameplay is praised as collaborative and protective · the effects model has never been proven · modern audiences are media-literate enough to separate games from reality · the dangers may be exaggerated — a press-driven moral panic.

Exam-Style Practice

The stepped question: short fact recalls first, then a 10–12 mark explain/discuss. Work at roughly a mark a minute.

Q1 · "Explain how video games make money for their producers. Refer to Fortnite. [12]"

Fortnite follows a GaaS (Games as a Service) model: Battle Royale is free at the point of sale, with revenue generated through microtransactions — skins, emotes and weapons bought with V-Bucks, the in-game currency exchanged for real money (~$1bn by July 2018). A fresh storefront every day and seasonal Battle Passes (~$9.50) keep players spending and playing; around 70% of players make in-game purchases. Further streams: the paid Save the World mode (£34.99–£49.99), live event tickets, merchandise, and the Epic Games Store funnelling players to other Epic-profiting titles. Cross-platform play and constant updates maximise the paying audience.

Q2 · "Explain why video games are regulated. Refer to Fortnite. [12]" — a balanced discussion

Regulation protects minors from harmful content: PEGI (Pan European Game Information) rates games by age (3–18) and content descriptors; since 2012 the system is UK law, administered by the VSC. Fortnite's 12 rating reflects mild violence and — crucially — in-game purchases, which concern parents more than the cartoon violence: children may feel pressured to buy skins, spend parents' money, or encounter strangers via in-game chat, while the WHO now recognises gaming addiction as a disorder. BALANCE: online ratings are hard to enforce, Fortnite's violence is milder than CoD or GTA, the effects model is unproven, and press coverage may amount to moral panic — so regulation matters, but audience media literacy and parental controls matter too.

Q3 · "Explain why users visit the Fortnite website. Refer to Uses and Gratifications. [12 — pick 2 areas, ~6 minutes]"

Information: users visit for official updates, patch notes, event schedules and tournament details — the trusted source, straight from Epic. Social relationships: the site encourages account sign-up to join a global community, links every social platform, promotes live events where players meet, and hosts tournaments — extending the game's social world beyond the game itself. (Personal identity — buying skins to express status — and entertainment — trailers and embedded videos — work equally well.)

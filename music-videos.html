Music Videos — Formation (Beyoncé, 2016)

Lead single from Lemonade, released the day before Beyoncé's 2016 Super Bowl performance, directed by Melina Matsoukas. Studied for Media Language and Representation — there is no Industry/Audience study for this text, and remember: the exam's Media Language question always uses an unseen music video, so Representation is what you'll actually be asked about on Formation itself.

Exam focus: the Media Language section below covers Goodwin's theory and general music video conventions — useful revision, but the exam question on an unseen text. The Representation section is where Formation itself will come up, so spend more time there.

Context in brief

The product

  • Lead single from Beyoncé's sixth album Lemonade (2016), available exclusively via streaming service Tidal.
  • Released a day before Beyoncé's Super Bowl 50 half-time performance — promotional synergy with a huge, pre-planned marketing moment (Formation World Tour advertised straight after).
  • Directed by Melina Matsoukas, known for bold colour, retro styling and socially-conscious visuals (Rihanna's "We Found Love").
  • Won multiple awards (MTV VMA Video of the Year, Grammy Best Music Video) and 230m+ YouTube views.
  • Set against the backdrop of Hurricane Katrina (New Orleans, 2005), the Black Lives Matter movement, and the 50th anniversary of the Black Panther Party.

Media Language

Applying Goodwin's music video theory

  • Andrew Goodwin: music videos show a relationship between lyrics and visuals (self-love lyrics — "I like my baby hair, with baby hair and afros" — matched with images of Blue Ivy's natural afro) and between music and visuals (bounce-genre rhythms match shots of New Orleans bounce culture).
  • Genre characteristics: Formation blends performance (choreographed dance sequences), narrative fragments (the flooded police car, the graveyard), and conceptual/symbolic imagery — Goodwin's point that categories are not mutually exclusive.
  • Demands of the record label — frequent close-ups and mid-shots of Beyoncé's face keep her star image central even in a video with a strong social message.
  • Intertextuality: the video references the Black Panther Party's uniform and the "X" formation from the 2016 Super Bowl show; the antebellum-era plantation setting intertextually references historical photography and cinema of the American South.

Codes & construction

Roland Barthes — the video is full of symbolic codes: the sinking police car symbolises the failure of the state during Katrina; the "X" dance formation symbolically echoes both a step-team routine and the Super Bowl staging; Beyoncé's positioning on top of the car connotes power over the disaster and the institutions that failed New Orleans.

Lévi-Strauss — meaning is built through binary oppositions: White vs Black, police vs Black communities, slavery vs freedom, and a "strong, independent woman" vs a woman sexualised through costume and choreography.

Applying Goodwin, there's a clear relationship between lyrics and visuals throughout Formation — for example the self-love lyric about "baby hair and afros" is directly illustrated by shots of Blue Ivy's natural hair, anchoring the song's meaning in Black pride. The video also mixes performance, narrative and conceptual modes rather than sitting in one category, which supports Goodwin's argument that music video genres overlap. Structurally (Lévi-Strauss), the video is built on binary oppositions — Black/white, police/community, slavery/freedom — that generate much of its political meaning.

Representation

Racial identity & Black history

  • The video is an almost exclusive celebration of Black culture and bodies: New Orleans bounce, Creole and Southern Black aesthetics, Mardi Gras Indian costume, black cowboys, step-team choreography.
  • The antebellum-era plantation scenes (men in period suits, Beyoncé in elaborate dress) reference the era of slavery, recontextualising imagery of the historical American South as a site of Black wealth, dignity and defiance rather than subjugation.
  • A young Black boy in a hoodie dances in front of a line of police officers, who eventually raise their hands in response — directly referencing the Black Lives Matter movement and cutting to graffiti reading "stop shooting us".
  • Beyoncé atop a sinking New Orleans police car recreates Hurricane Katrina flooding — she uses her weight to sink it further, a symbolic act of power over an institution associated with the state's failure to protect Black communities.

Empowerment, gender & the plantation scenes

  • Beyoncé and her all-Black dance crew are shown as a unified, powerful collective — jewellery, fine fabrics and grand plantation-house settings connote wealth, defiance and freedom rather than servitude.
  • In the "black attire" scene, Beyoncé sits surrounded by men in formal suits, then flicks the camera off — a direct, confrontational address that subverts expectations of a demure, passive female star image.
  • But the video is also criticised for contradiction: sections show Beyoncé and her dancers in highly sexualised outfits and choreography, arguably reinforcing the objectification it elsewhere seems to challenge — does a female director (Matsoukas) negate the male gaze, or not?
  • Lyrics like "when he f*** me good I take his ass to Red Lobster" invert traditional gender/power dynamics in relationships — she rewards good performance, flipping patriarchal dominance.

Debates & criticisms — evaluate both sides

Jean Baudrillard — postmodernism: is the video a genuine, specific commentary on real events (Katrina, BLM), encouraging audiences to engage with these issues — or is it a hyperreal collection of images, detached from reality and repackaged to sell Beyoncé's brand and the Formation World Tour?

Critics note none of the New Orleans footage was actually filmed there (it was reused from a 2012 documentary), and that luxury imagery (Givenchy, expensive jewellery) sits uneasily beside references to real tragedy and protest. Others argue Beyoncé "waited until Black politics was so undeniably commercial that she could make a market out of it" (a reading you should be able to summarise, not quote at length).

Paul Gilroy — otherness: the video can be read as challenging racial hierarchy by placing a Black woman in a position of power and dominance, but may also risk reinforcing a sense of racial "otherness" and binary division rather than transcending it.

bell hooks — feminism: does Formation represent a genuine political commitment to intersectional feminism, or is feminism being used here as a marketable "lifestyle choice"? Consider the contrasting, sometimes contradictory representations within the same video.

A Baudrillardian reading might argue Formation is a hyperreal assemblage of powerful signifiers — Katrina, BLM, plantation imagery — stripped of their original context and repurposed to sell Beyoncé's star brand and upcoming tour, especially given that none of the "New Orleans" footage was filmed there. A more sympathetic reading (closer to Hall's idea of active audience decoding) argues the video invites audiences to genuinely engage with real social and historical issues through its symbolism, even while promoting Beyoncé commercially — the two readings aren't mutually exclusive, and a strong exam answer would explore this tension rather than settling on only one.
